# How to actually stand out

Strategy notes for the submission — separate from the answer itself.

---

## What the median submission will look like

Predict the field and you can route around it. Most submissions will:

- Pick Challenge 1 or 5 (they're the most tractable)
- Be slides, or a Figma mock, narrated
- Say: voice-first, vernacular, WhatsApp, proactive nudges, no screens, gamification, AI coach
- List features rather than make an argument
- Contain zero numbers
- Contain nothing the founder could disagree with
- Not mention anything specific about Hunar

That set is competent and interchangeable. Every differentiator below is cheap.

---

## The five levers, in order of return on effort

### 1. Talk to one real delivery rider. Thirty minutes. Do this today.

Highest-leverage thing available to you, by a wide margin.

Order something, or go to a Blinkit/Zepto/Swiggy pickup point. Ask a rider four questions:

1. What's the most annoying part of your day that has nothing to do with traffic?
2. Last time your payment was wrong — what did you do?
3. When did you last call support, and did it help?
4. If you could change one thing about the app, what?

Then **open your video with fifteen seconds of his actual voice**, or quote him by name once. It reframes
everything after it from opinion into evidence.

Why this matters for *this* company specifically: Hunar's founders ran a real recruitment agency for about two
years before building any AI, precisely to get ground truth about frontline hiring and attrition
([Inc42](https://inc42.com/startups/why-hunar-ai-thinks-indias-frontline-workforce-needs-ai-agents/), *rephrased
for compliance with licensing restrictions*). Doing thirty minutes of the same thing is a direct demonstration
that you'd operate the way they do. Almost nobody else will bother.

### 2. Say one thing that could be wrong

A product answer nobody can disagree with hasn't said anything. The three arguable claims in this draft:

- The agent needs a literal **spending limit** — delegated financial authority, not just intelligence
- It should sometimes tell a driver to **go work for a competitor today**
- Ops **headcount falls** and the customer should be told in month one

Say them plainly and defend them. A founder would far rather argue with you than agree with you politely.

### 3. Put numbers on it

You're auditioning for a **Forward Deployed** role: you sit in front of an enterprise buyer. Show you can build
the case — ₹30 Cr attrition exposure, ₹12 Cr defensible, ₹2–4 Cr contract. State your assumptions out loud so
they're attackable. Notice that Hunar itself talks in outcome numbers (10M+ engagements, 90% faster hiring cycle,
15% first-month productivity lift), so this is the register they think in.

### 4. Demonstrate, don't describe

Don't say "the agent would talk to him." **Play the conversation.** Build it in
[Hunar's playground](https://playground.hunar.ai/) or ElevenLabs. Forty-five seconds of working audio beats four
minutes of narration, and it means you've used their product before your interview.

### 5. Know their engineering, not just their pitch

Hunar publishes unusually specific detail: models for handling interruptions and finding the right *resumption
point* mid-conversation, a deliberately engineered 700–900ms response window, and a maker-checker second agent
that reviews completed calls to hold accuracy at 90–95% ([hunar.ai](https://hunar.ai/), *rephrased*).

One sentence engaging with that puts you in a different bracket. The best one available:

> "Ninety-to-ninety-five percent is a great number for extracting data from a finished hiring call. Point that at
> an agent that closes deliveries and moves money across four hundred million events a year and the absolute error
> volume is enormous — and a checker that reviews the call *afterwards* doesn't help when the agent has to act
> mid-conversation. So: tiered confidence, and when the agent is wrong, the company eats it, never the driver."

That single answer shows you read their engineering, understood why operations is a harder regime than hiring, and
proposed a design response.

## Two things not to do

**Don't answer two challenges.** The brief says choose one. Two decent answers reads as hedging; one excellent one
reads as judgement. (I suggested this last time — it was wrong.)

**Don't build a polished UI prototype.** The brief explicitly says don't think in screens. A beautiful mock quietly
argues against your own thesis. At most, five seconds of a phone showing a *receipt*, after the fact.

## The four questions a founder will actually probe

Prepare one crisp answer each. These are the pressure points in the design, and being ready for them is worth more
than another minute of video.

**1. "This sounds like a friend, not a manager. What happens when the driver is bad at his job?"**
The gap in most submissions. Answer: today's platforms already punish — silently, via algorithmic
deprioritisation, with nothing to appeal. My agent makes punishment *spoken, specific and appealable*. The
advocacy is only credible because the confrontation is real. (§4 of the answer.)

**2. "We're paying for this. Why does it work for the driver?"**
Attrition is your largest controllable cost — thirty crore of annual exposure at your scale. The only thing that
reduces it is him believing the agent is on his side, and belief requires visibly acting against you sometimes. The
loyalty flip *is* the ROI mechanism. It isn't charity you tolerate; it's the thing you're buying.

**3. "How is this different from our dispatch system with a voice on top?"**
The scorecard. This agent is reviewed on his earnings per hour, his ninety-day retention and his progression —
never on throughput. Loyalty in a system prompt is cosplay; loyalty in the objective function is structural. If
throughput is on the agent's scorecard, you've rebuilt the dispatcher with a nicer voice.

**4. "Which part of this is most likely to fail?"**
The authority grant. No enterprise hands software a budget on day one — which is exactly why the wedge is scoped to
the single exception where the ROI is impossible to argue with. And the harder version of the same fight: the
customer will want throughput on the agent's scorecard. Concede audit rights and reporting. Never concede the
objective function.

## On format

I considered making the whole video a conversation with the agent — no narration at all, the agent onboards *you*,
the viewer. It's memorable and it's the boldest fit for a voice-AI company. I'd reject it: five minutes isn't
enough to carry both a gimmick and a thesis, and if the conceit wobbles you lose the argument with it.

The version in the script is the sensible bold: **real generated voice at both ends, narration in the middle.** The
close is delivered in the agent's voice rather than yours, which bookends the demo and shows the product one last
time for free. Cheap, and nobody else will do it.
