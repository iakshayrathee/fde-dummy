### [1:08] — 25s

He already *has* a manager. It's the dispatch algorithm — it sets his workload, scores him, pays him, can deactivate
him.

But look at which half of management got automated. A manager allocates, measures and punishes. A manager also
coaches, explains, advocates and protects. The gig economy automated the first half brilliantly and simply deleted
the second.

It kept the whip and threw away the hand.

---

### [1:33] — 47s

So I'm not adding a manager. I'm restoring the half that was thrown away. Three things do that.

Memory. He tells it once — that this gate is locked after eight. And it propagates: none of the other forty-nine
thousand ever hit that wall again.

Authority. And the engineering version of that word: the reason this doesn't exist isn't that the models weren't good
enough. It's that the agent has **no write access**. Waiving a penalty is a write to your payments system. Authority,
inside an enterprise, is an API scope and a spend limit.

And accountability. You can't make an agent loyal with a system prompt. So put loyalty in the scorecard — earnings
per hour, retention, whether he got promoted. Not throughput. **The agent's boss is the driver.**

---

### [2:20] — 78s · THE BRIEF SAID FORGET THE APP. THIS IS THAT.

None of what follows is a better version of something in the app.

He never logs in. Ever. His voice is his identity — attendance, delivery confirmation, payments. Login is the most
literacy-hostile object in enterprise software. For a man who can't read the field label, *forgot password* isn't a
link, it's the end of his workday.

The shift is dead. He doesn't accept a block of time — he says *I need fifteen hundred today and I have to finish by
six*, and the agent solves backwards and tells him the odds. He stops being scheduled and starts being served.

Agents negotiate with each other. Ravi's agent and Suresh's swap two stops directly, no ops involved, because both
drivers end up better off. Central dispatch optimises one objective — the company's. A market of driver-aligned
agents finds allocations dispatch can't see.

And the one that matters most. Four hundred riders each lose eighteen minutes a day at the same warehouse.
Individually, not one has a complaint worth making, and nobody has ever seen the pattern. The agent does — so it files
one case, with data, for four hundred people.

Operational friction only — never pay rates. That limit is what protects it.

Fifty thousand powerless people, collectively legible for the first time.

### [3:46] — 15s

An agent that only defends him is a lawyer, not a manager. These platforms already punish — silently. Orders dry up,
nobody says why, nothing to appeal. So punishment becomes spoken, specific and appealable. Termination stays
human. Always.

### [4:02] — 25s

They'll ask why they can't build this in-house.

They can build the voice — voice is commoditising. What they can't build is **neutrality**. An in-house agent is the
company's agent by construction. No rider will believe the thing built by the people who pay him is on his side — and
he'd be right.

Neutrality is the product.

And to be clear: I've just described a three-year platform. I'd ship one exception handler. One wedge. And note what
